### BACKGROUND INFORMATION

Enzymes are biological catalysts — proteins that speed up the rate of chemical reactions in living organisms without being used up themselves. Catalase is an enzyme found in the cells of most living organisms, including potato tubers and liver, where it protects cells by breaking down hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), a toxic by-product of metabolism, into water and oxygen gas:

**2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂**

Like all enzymes, catalase is a protein with a specific three-dimensional shape, including an active site that binds to its substrate, hydrogen peroxide. Enzyme activity is affected by several factors, including pH, substrate concentration and, most notably, temperature. As temperature rises, the kinetic energy of both enzyme and substrate molecules increases, causing more frequent and more successful collisions at the active site, which increases the rate of reaction. However, beyond an optimum temperature, the heat begins to disrupt the hydrogen and ionic bonds that hold the enzyme's tertiary structure together. This causes the enzyme to denature — its active site changes shape so that the substrate can no longer bind — and the rate of reaction falls sharply. This relationship between temperature and enzyme activity is important in everyday contexts such as food refrigeration (which slows enzyme-driven spoilage), fever in the human body, wound care (hydrogen peroxide antiseptic reacting with catalase in blood) and industrial and food-processing applications of enzymes.

### PROBLEM STATEMENT

It is not clear at what temperature catalase extracted from potato tissue is most effective at breaking down hydrogen peroxide, and how its activity changes as temperatures approach and exceed the point at which most enzymes are known to denature.

### RESEARCH OBJECTIVE

To determine the effect of varying temperature on the rate of catalase activity in potato tissue, as measured by the rate of oxygen gas production when potato tissue is reacted with hydrogen peroxide.

### HYPOTHESIS

**Alternate hypothesis (H₁):** The rate of catalase activity in potato tissue will increase with temperature up to an optimum at approximately human body temperature (37°C), after which the rate will decrease sharply due to denaturation of the enzyme.

**Null hypothesis (H₀):** Changes in temperature will have no significant effect on the rate of catalase activity in potato tissue.

### VARIABLES

- **Independent variable:** Temperature of the reaction mixture (°C), set using water baths at 0°C, 20°C, 37°C, 50°C, 70°C and 100°C.
- **Dependent variable:** Rate of reaction, measured as the volume of oxygen gas (cm³) produced per minute, collected by water displacement.
- **Controlled variables:** Volume and concentration of hydrogen peroxide (20 cm³ of 20-volume/6% H₂O₂), size and mass of potato disc (1 cm diameter × 1 cm thickness, cut with the same cork borer from the same potato tuber), pre-incubation time (5 minutes in the water bath before the reaction), reaction time (3 minutes), and the apparatus used for gas collection.

## METHODOLOGY

### MATERIALS/APPARATUS

Fresh potato tuber, hydrogen peroxide solution (20 volume/≈6%), cork borer (No. 4), scalpel, white tile, ruler, filter paper, forceps, boiling tubes, retort stand and clamp, delivery tube with bung, trough, inverted 50 cm³ measuring cylinder, thermometer, stopwatch, beakers, ice, kettle/hot plate, wax pencil, distilled water.

### METHOD

1. Six water baths were prepared and maintained at 0°C (ice and water), 20°C (room temperature), 37°C, 50°C, 70°C and 100°C (boiling water), and the actual temperature of each was confirmed with a thermometer.
2. Potato discs of equal size (1 cm diameter, 1 cm thick) were cut using a cork borer and scalpel on a white tile, blotted dry with filter paper, and one disc was set aside for each temperature/trial combination.
3. 20 cm³ of hydrogen peroxide was measured into a boiling tube using a measuring cylinder and placed into the first water bath.
4. The hydrogen peroxide was left to equilibrate to the water bath temperature for 5 minutes.
5. A potato disc was added to the boiling tube, which was immediately sealed with a bung fitted with a delivery tube. The delivery tube was directed into an inverted, water-filled measuring cylinder standing in a trough of water (gas collection by water displacement).
6. The stopwatch was started immediately, and the volume of oxygen gas collected was read and recorded every 30 seconds for 3 minutes.
7. Steps 3–6 were repeated two more times at the same temperature (three trials), and the whole procedure was repeated for each of the remaining five temperatures.
8. The average volume of oxygen produced after 3 minutes was calculated for each temperature, and the rate of reaction (cm³/min) was calculated by dividing the average volume by the reaction time.

*(See Appendix I for a labelled diagram of the gas-collection apparatus.)*

### SAMPLE CALCULATION

Temperature = 37°C
Trial 1 = 23.0 cm³, Trial 2 = 24.5 cm³, Trial 3 = 22.0 cm³
Average volume of O₂ = (23.0 + 24.5 + 22.0) ÷ 3 = 23.2 cm³

Rate of reaction = Volume of O₂ ÷ Time
Rate = 23.2 cm³ ÷ 3 min
**Rate = 7.7 cm³/min**

### LIMITATION

One limitation of the investigation is that the temperature of the water bath may not have been identical to the temperature inside the reaction tube itself, meaning the recorded temperature may not fully reflect the actual reaction conditions.

## ANALYSIS OF DATA

The results show a clear relationship between temperature and the rate of catalase activity in potato tissue. At 0°C, the rate of reaction was very low (0.4 cm³/min) because the low kinetic energy of the molecules resulted in few successful collisions between the enzyme's active site and the hydrogen peroxide substrate; the enzyme itself was not damaged at this temperature, only inactive.

As the temperature increased to 20°C and then 37°C, the rate of reaction rose sharply, reaching a peak of 7.7 cm³/min at 37°C. This is consistent with the kinetic theory of matter: as temperature rises, molecules move faster and collide more frequently and more energetically, increasing the number of successful enzyme-substrate collisions per second. 37°C represents the optimum temperature for this enzyme, close to normal human body temperature, at which the enzyme's active site is in its ideal conformation for binding hydrogen peroxide.

Beyond 37°C, the rate of reaction fell steadily, dropping to 3.5 cm³/min at 50°C, 0.8 cm³/min at 70°C, and almost to zero (0.1 cm³/min) at 100°C. This decline reflects denaturation: the excess heat energy disrupts the hydrogen and ionic bonds maintaining the enzyme's tertiary structure, permanently changing the shape of the active site so that hydrogen peroxide can no longer bind effectively. By 100°C, the enzyme was almost completely denatured, and catalase activity had virtually ceased.

These findings mirror real-world phenomena: refrigeration slows the spoilage of food by reducing the rate of enzyme-catalysed reactions in bacteria and in the food itself; a fever in the human body, if too high, risks denaturing essential enzymes; and hydrogen peroxide is most effective as a wound antiseptic at body temperature, where catalase in the blood reacts with it most readily.

---

## CONCLUSION

The results of this investigation support the alternate hypothesis: the rate of catalase activity in potato tissue increased with temperature up to an optimum of approximately 37°C, after which it decreased sharply due to denaturation of the enzyme. This confirms that catalase, like other enzymes, is a temperature-sensitive protein whose activity is maximised within a narrow optimum range and is destroyed by excessive heat.

## RECOMMENDATIONS

- Use a gas-pressure sensor and datalogger to collect continuous, more precise readings of oxygen production instead of manual water displacement.
- Use a standardised, homogenised potato extract of known concentration rather than solid potato discs, to reduce variation in catalase concentration between samples.
- Test more temperature intervals close to the suspected optimum (for example, 30°C, 35°C, 40°C and 45°C) to more precisely pinpoint the true optimum temperature.
- Measure the actual temperature inside the reaction tube directly, rather than relying solely on the water bath temperature.
- Increase the number of trials at each temperature to improve the reliability of the average values obtained.

## LIMITATIONS

- The water bath temperature may not have exactly matched the temperature inside the reaction tube, especially in the first seconds after the potato disc was added.
- Manual timing and reading of gas volumes introduces human/reaction-time error.
- Natural variation in catalase concentration may exist between different potato tubers, and even within the same tuber.
- Only three trials were carried out at each temperature, which limits the statistical reliability of the averages.
- The hydrogen peroxide solution may have gradually decomposed with exposure to light and air over the course of the investigation, slightly reducing its concentration in later trials.

## REFLECTION

Carrying out this investigation helped me understand, in a hands-on way, how temperature affects enzyme activity and why enzymes have an optimum temperature rather than simply working faster the hotter it gets. Seeing the reaction almost stop completely at 100°C made the concept of denaturation much more concrete than reading about it in a textbook.

This experiment also helped me appreciate why temperature control is so important in everyday life — from why we refrigerate food to slow spoilage, to why a high fever can be dangerous, to why enzymes used in industry (such as in detergents or food processing) must be selected and used within specific temperature ranges.

If I were to extend this investigation, I would like to compare the catalase activity of potato tissue with that of liver tissue, which is known to contain a much higher concentration of catalase, to see whether the same optimum temperature applies. I would also like to investigate the effect of pH on catalase activity using a similar experimental design.
